Assume the rod is homogeneous, isotropic, linear‑elastic, and experiences only small axial strains; one‑dimensional stress wave theory applies.  
Material properties: Young’s modulus E = 200 GPa, density ρ = 7850 kg/m³.  
Geometry: length L = 1.5 m, circular cross‑section diameter d = 16 mm, area A = πd²/4 = 2.01 × 10⁻⁴ m².  
Static loading: axial force P_s = 800 N applied at the fixed end, producing uniform static stress σ_s = P_s/A ≈ 4.0 MPa.  
Impact loading: mass m = 81.5 kg (derived from weight 800 N) dropped from height h = 0.15 m onto the free end, giving impact velocity v₀ = √(2gh) ≈ 1.715 m/s.  
Wave speed in the rod: c = √(E/ρ) ≈ 5047 m/s.  
Acoustic impedance of the rod: Z = ρ c A ≈ 7.96 × 10³ N·s/m.  
Peak stress generated by the impact (elastic wave): σ_i = ρ c v₀ ≈ 68 MPa (equivalently Z v₀/A).  
Total peak axial stress: σ_max = σ_s + σ_i ≈ 72 MPa.  
Corresponding peak strain: ε_max = σ_max/E ≈ 3.6 × 10⁻⁴.  
Maximum axial displacement at the impact point during the first half‑wave: δ_max = ε_max L ≈ 5.4 × 10⁻⁴ m = 0.54 mm.  
Travel time of the stress wave along the rod: t_c = L/c ≈ 2.97 × 10⁻⁴ s = 0.297 ms.  
Additional assumptions: the impact is perfectly elastic (no energy loss), friction and damping are neglected, and the rod’s own mass is accounted for only through its density in the wave speed and impedance calculations.
