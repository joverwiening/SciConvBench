Assume the beam undergoes small deformations, material behavior is linear‑elastic, and the Euler‑Bernoulli beam theory applies.  
Assume the beam material is homogeneous, isotropic, and has a constant rectangular (or otherwise uniform) cross‑section along its length.  
Neglect shear deformation, rotary inertia, and any axial loads.  
The beam is a cantilever of length L = 1200 mm fixed at x = 0 and free at x = L.  
A vertical point load P = 800 N acts downward at the free end (x = L).  
Young’s modulus of the material is E = 200 000 MPa (200 000 N/mm²).  
The second moment of area of the cross‑section is I = 2 500 000 mm⁴.  

The internal shear force is constant: V(x) = ‑P for 0 ≤ x ≤ L.  
The bending moment distribution is M(x) = ‑P (L ‑ x) for 0 ≤ x ≤ L.  

The slope of the elastic axis is θ(x) = P x (2L ‑ x) / (2 E I).  
The transverse deflection is v(x) = P x² (3L ‑ x) / (6 E I).  

At the free end (x = L):  
Tip deflection δ = v(L) = P L³ / (3 E I) ≈ 0.922 mm.  
Tip slope θ(L) = P L² / (2 E I) ≈ 1.152 × 10⁻³ rad (≈ 0.066°).  

The maximum bending moment occurs at the fixed support: M_max = P L = 960 000 N·mm.  
The corresponding extreme bending stress is σ_max = M_max c / I, where c is the distance from the neutral axis to the outermost fiber of the cross‑section (σ_max expressed symbolically because c is not specified).  

These governing relations and computed response quantities fully describe the cantilever beam’s behavior under the given end point load.
