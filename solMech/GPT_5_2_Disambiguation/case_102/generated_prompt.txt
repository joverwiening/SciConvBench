Use small-deformation, linear-elastic axial deformation for the rod.
Use N–mm–MPa consistent units throughout.
Model the rod as a uniform axial spring with stiffness k = AE/L.
Neglect the rod mass.
Model the impact as a dropped weight that sticks at contact (no rebound).
Assume the only load is the dropped weight W during impact, with no separate initial static axial load P0 = 0.
Given L = 800 mm.
Given d = 25 mm.
Use cross-sectional area A = πd^2/4 = 490.9 mm^2.
Given Young’s modulus E = 200,000 MPa = 200,000 N/mm^2.
Given dropped weight force W = 1500 N.
Given drop height h = 200 mm.
Let δmax be the maximum axial shortening (deflection) of the rod at peak force.
Relate peak force to deflection by δmax = Pmax L/(AE).
Write energy conservation from release to maximum deflection as W(h + δmax) = U, where U is the rod strain energy at peak force.
Use axial strain energy U = Pmax^2 L/(2AE).
Substitute δmax into the energy equation to obtain W(h + Pmax L/(AE)) = Pmax^2 L/(2AE).
Rearrange into a quadratic in Pmax: Pmax^2 − 2WPmax − (2AEW h)/L = 0.
Solve for the physically admissible (positive) root: Pmax = W + sqrt(W^2 + (2AEW h)/L).
Compute AE/L = (490.9×200,000)/800 = 122,725 N/mm.
Compute (2AEW h)/L = 2×1500×200×(AE/L) = 73,635,000,000 N^2.
Compute Pmax = 1500 + sqrt(1500^2 + 73,635,000,000) = 272,800 N (approximately).
Compute maximum normal stress as σmax = Pmax/A = 272,800/490.9 = 556 MPa (approximately).
