Use small-deformation, linear-elastic solid mechanics.
Model the member as a prismatic beam with a rectangular cross-section of width b and height h.
Use Euler–Bernoulli internal-resultant relations to obtain the internal transverse shear force V(x) from the applied transverse loading.
Assume the transverse shear stress distribution is governed by elementary beam shear-stress theory (no Timoshenko shear deformation).
Assume Saint-Venant type stress distribution away from load application points and supports so that beam shear formulas apply.
Assume the shear stress is uniformly distributed across the width b at a given vertical coordinate y (i.e., τ varies with y but not across b).
Assume material isotropy and homogeneity; Poisson effects are not needed for the shear-stress calculation.
Use consistent MPa-based units (e.g., N and mm so that stress is N/mm² = MPa).

Relevant response quantity to report for transverse shear in a rectangular beam is τ(y) (transverse shear stress as a function of depth coordinate y) and its maximum value τmax at the neutral axis.

Part (a) governing relation for maximum transverse shear stress in a rectangular section:
Compute cross-sectional area A = b h.
Compute internal shear force V at the section of interest.
Compute maximum transverse shear stress as τmax = 1.5 V / A.
State that τmax occurs at the neutral axis (y = 0 for a symmetric rectangle about its centroid).

Part (b) governing relation for transverse shear stress distribution through the depth:
Define y as the vertical coordinate measured from the centroidal neutral axis, positive upward, with −h/2 ≤ y ≤ +h/2.
Use the beam shear formula τ(y) = V Q(y) / (I b).
Use the second moment of area about the centroidal axis I = b h^3 / 12.
Define Q(y) as the first moment of area of the portion of the cross-section above the level y about the neutral axis:
Let A′(y) be the area from y to +h/2, so A′(y) = b (h/2 − y).
Let ȳ′(y) be the distance from the neutral axis to the centroid of A′(y), so ȳ′(y) = (y + h/2)/2.
Compute Q(y) = A′(y) ȳ′(y) = b (h/2 − y) (y + h/2)/2 = b (h^2/4 − y^2)/2.
Substitute into τ(y) to obtain τ(y) = V [b (h^2/4 − y^2)/2] / [(b h^3/12) b] = (3V / (2 b h)) [1 − (2y/h)^2].
State that τ(y) is parabolic, τ(±h/2) = 0 at the top and bottom surfaces, and τ(0) = 3V/(2 b h) = 1.5 V/A at the neutral axis.
