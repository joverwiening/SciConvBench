The problem is a dropped weight impact on a linear-elastic, small-deformation bar.
The bar is made of structural steel.
The bar has a circular cross-section with a diameter of 30 mm.
The bar length is 1200 mm.
There is no pre-applied static axial load.
A weight of 500 N is dropped onto one end of the bar from a height of 200 mm.
Assume the impact is perfectly inelastic (the weight does not rebound and remains in contact with the bar).
Assume the bar is initially unstressed and at rest.
Assume the bar's supports are frictionless and allow free axial deformation.
Neglect damping and wave propagation effects within the bar for a simplified quasi-static analysis.
The relevant response quantity is the maximum dynamic stress induced in the bar.
The governing relation uses conservation of energy: the potential energy loss of the weight equals the strain energy stored in the bar at maximum deflection.
The potential energy loss is m*g*(h + δ_max), where m = W/g, g is gravitational acceleration, h is drop height, and δ_max is the maximum dynamic deflection.
The strain energy in the bar is (1/2) * (AE/L) * δ_max^2, where A is the cross-sectional area, E is Young's modulus for steel, and L is the bar length.
Equating these gives (AE/L) * δ_max^2 - 2W*δ_max - 2W*h = 0, which is solved for δ_max.
The maximum dynamic stress is then σ_max = (E * δ_max) / L.
The static stress for a gradually applied weight W is σ_static = W / A.
The dynamic load factor (DLF) is the ratio σ_max / σ_static.
