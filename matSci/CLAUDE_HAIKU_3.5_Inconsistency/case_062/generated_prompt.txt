The thermal-shock resistance decreases as the thermal expansion coefficient (α) increases.

When α increases, the denominator of the thermal-shock parameter R grows larger, which directly reduces R.

Physically, a higher thermal expansion coefficient means the material experiences more significant dimensional changes when temperature rapidly shifts, creating larger internal thermal stresses.

These larger internal stresses increase the likelihood of crack initiation and propagation, which reduces the material's ability to withstand sudden temperature changes.

The reduced thermal-shock resistance occurs because:
- Larger α leads to more significant thermal strain
- More thermal strain creates higher internal mechanical stress
- Higher internal stress increases the probability of material failure
- The material becomes less capable of tolerating rapid temperature transitions

The relationship highlights why materials with lower thermal expansion coefficients (like certain ceramics with controlled composition) tend to have better thermal-shock resistance.
