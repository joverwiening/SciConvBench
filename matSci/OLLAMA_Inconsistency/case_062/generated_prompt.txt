The thermal‑shock parameter R is proportional to the product of the material’s fracture strength σ_f and its thermal conductivity k, and inversely proportional to the product of Young’s modulus E and the coefficient of thermal expansion α.  
A high fracture strength σ_f means the ceramic can sustain larger stresses before cracking, which raises R and improves thermal‑shock resistance.  
High thermal conductivity k spreads heat quickly through the material, reducing temperature gradients and the associated thermal stresses, also increasing R.  
A high Young’s modulus E makes the material stiffer, so a given thermal strain produces larger stresses, which lowers R and reduces resistance.  
The coefficient of thermal expansion α measures how much the material expands or contracts for a given temperature change; a larger α generates larger strains for the same temperature jump.  
Because R contains α in the denominator, increasing α directly reduces the value of R, meaning the ceramic becomes more prone to thermal‑shock failure.  
Physically, a higher α causes greater differential expansion between hot and cold regions, creating higher internal stresses that can exceed the fracture strength more easily.  
Therefore, to improve thermal‑shock resistance, designers seek ceramics with low α, high σ_f, high k, and moderate E, balancing stiffness with the ability to accommodate thermal strain.  
In summary, increasing the coefficient of thermal expansion weakens thermal‑shock resistance because it amplifies the thermal strain that drives cracking.
