Q: In the expression R ∝ σ_f k/(Eα), α is in the denominator, so increasing α decreases R, which would worsen thermal-shock resistance, not improve it.

Qualitatively, a higher thermal expansion coefficient means the material “wants” to change size more for a given temperature change.
During a thermal shock, temperature gradients develop, so different regions try to expand/contract by different amounts.
That mismatch creates larger thermally induced strains and therefore larger stresses when α is higher.
Larger thermal stresses make crack initiation and growth more likely, so resistance to thermal shock generally decreases as α increases.

Any conclusion that “increasing α improves thermal-shock resistance” must be relying on something outside this simple proportionality (for example, α changing together with other properties like strength, toughness, modulus, or thermal conductivity), but holding σ_f, k, and E fixed, increasing α is detrimental.
A: Since \(R \propto \dfrac{\sigma_f\, k}{E\,\alpha}\), increasing the thermal expansion coefficient \(\alpha\) (with \(\sigma_f\), \(k\), and \(E\) held fixed) **decreases \(R\)**, so it **reduces thermal-shock resistance**.

Qualitatively: a larger \(\alpha\) means the ceramic undergoes more thermal strain for a given temperature change. Under thermal shock, temperature gradients cause different parts of the body to try to expand/contract by different amounts; higher \(\alpha\) increases this mismatch, producing higher thermal stresses and making cracking more likely.
