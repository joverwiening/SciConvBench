The thermal-shock parameter R is proportional to σ_f * k / (E * α), where σ_f is fracture strength, k is thermal conductivity, E is Young's modulus, and α is the coefficient of thermal expansion.
The conclusion that increasing α improves thermal-shock resistance is based on the given proportionality.
Qualitatively, this conclusion is counter-intuitive because a higher α typically means a material expands or contracts more for a given temperature change, generating higher thermal stresses.
The discussion must resolve this apparent contradiction by examining the full context of the parameter R.
The parameter R likely represents a critical temperature difference ΔT_c causing fracture, derived from a balance of thermal stress and material strength.
In such a derivation, the thermal stress σ_thermal is proportional to E * α * ΔT.
Fracture occurs when σ_thermal equals the fracture strength σ_f, so σ_f = E * α * ΔT_c.
Solving for the critical temperature difference gives ΔT_c ∝ σ_f / (E * α).
Incorporating thermal conductivity k affects the rate of heat transfer and the severity of the temperature gradient.
For a given heat flux or cooling rate, a higher k reduces the temperature gradient, lowering the effective ΔT experienced by the material.
Therefore, the full thermal-shock resistance parameter often includes k, resulting in R ∝ σ_f * k / (E * α).
The statement "increasing α improves thermal-shock resistance" is only true if all other parameters (σ_f, k, E) remain constant, which is not typical in real materials.
In reality, α is a material property that often correlates with other properties; increasing α usually decreases thermal-shock resistance because it directly increases thermal stress.
