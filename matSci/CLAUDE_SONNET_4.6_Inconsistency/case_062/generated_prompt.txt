The thermal-shock resistance parameter is R ∝ σ_f k / (E α).
Identify α (coefficient of thermal expansion) as appearing in the denominator of R.
Since α is in the denominator, R is inversely proportional to α.
Conclude that as α increases, R decreases, meaning thermal-shock resistance gets worse.
The original conclusion that "increasing α improves thermal-shock resistance" is therefore incorrect.
Explain qualitatively that a higher α means the material expands or contracts more per degree of temperature change.
Greater dimensional change per degree generates larger internal thermal stresses when a temperature gradient exists.
Larger thermal stresses make it easier to initiate or propagate cracks, reducing resistance to thermal shock.
Conversely, a material with a lower α undergoes less strain per degree of temperature change.
Less strain means smaller induced stresses for the same temperature gradient, so the material survives thermal cycling better.
Use the example that fused silica has a very low α (≈ 0.5 × 10⁻⁶ /°C) and excellent thermal-shock resistance, consistent with this analysis.
Use the example that ordinary soda-lime glass has a higher α (≈ 9 × 10⁻⁶ /°C) and much poorer thermal-shock resistance, also consistent.
State the corrected qualitative conclusion: to improve thermal-shock resistance, α should be minimized, not increased.
