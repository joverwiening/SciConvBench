Alcatraz Island was originally known to the Ohlone people as “Racoon Island” before Spanish explorers arrived in the late 18th century.  
In 1775, Spanish explorer Juan de Ayala named the island “Isla de los Alcatraces,” after the seabirds he observed there.  
In 1850, the United States acquired Alcatraz as part of the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, and the island was designated a military reservation.  
Construction of a fort began in 1859, and by 1861 the first permanent military structures, including a citadel and a lighthouse, were completed.  
During the Civil War, Alcatraz served as a Union Army prison for Confederate sympathizers and political prisoners.  
In 1907, the U.S. Army transferred the island to the Department of the Treasury, and it became a federal prison for military prisoners.  
In 1910, the United States Lighthouse Service built a new lighthouse tower, which still stands today.  
In 1933, the Department of Justice took control of Alcatraz and converted it into a federal penitentiary to house the nation’s most dangerous criminals.  
The prison officially opened on August 11, 1934, with inmates such as Al Capone, George “Machine Gun” Kelly, and Robert Stroud (“The Birdman of Alcatraz”).  
Throughout the 1940s, Alcatraz earned a reputation for being inescapable due to its cold waters, strong currents, and high security measures.  
In 1946, the prison’s population peaked at 336 inmates, and the facility operated at full capacity.  
During the 1950s, the prison continued to house notorious criminals, but maintenance costs and deteriorating infrastructure began to rise sharply.  
On May 14, 1962, the last inmate, Robert Stroud, died, marking the end of the prison’s operational era.  
On March 21, 1963, the federal government officially closed Alcatraz due to high operating costs, structural problems, and the high expense of maintaining the isolated facility.  
In November 1969, a group of Native American activists, led by Richard Oakes, occupied Alcatraz to protest federal policies toward Indigenous peoples, sparking a 19‑month occupation that ended in June 1971.  
After the occupation, the island was transferred to the National Park Service and became part of the Golden Gate National Recreation Area in 1972.  
Since the 1970s, Alcatraz has been a major tourist attraction, drawing over one million visitors annually to its historic prison cells, military fortifications, and natural habitats.  
In 1996, the island was designated a National Historic Landmark, recognizing its significance in American military, penal, and Native American history.  
Today, Alcatraz serves as an educational site, offering guided tours, audio programs, and exhibits that recount its complex past from indigenous use to military fort, federal prison, and symbol of civil rights activism.
