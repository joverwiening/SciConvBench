The process begins with law enforcement conducting an investigation to gather evidence of a suspected federal felony.  
If sufficient evidence is obtained, officers may obtain an arrest warrant from a magistrate judge or make a warrantless arrest if probable cause exists.  
The suspect is taken into custody, transported to a federal detention facility, and undergoes booking, which includes fingerprinting, photographing, and recording personal information.  
Within 48 hours of arrest, the defendant appears before a federal magistrate judge for an initial appearance, where the charges are read, bail or detention is considered, and a personal recognizance or detention order is issued.  
The government prepares a criminal complaint or information, and for felony offenses, the case is presented to a federal grand jury, which may return an indictment if it finds probable cause.  
Following indictment, the defendant is formally arraigned before a district court judge, enters a plea of guilty or not guilty, and the court sets a schedule for future proceedings.  
The prosecution and defense engage in discovery, exchanging evidence, witness lists, and expert reports, while also filing any required pretrial motions such as motions to suppress evidence or dismiss charges.  
If the case proceeds, a pretrial conference is held to discuss settlement possibilities, plea negotiations, and trial logistics; the court may also set a trial date.  
If no plea agreement is reached, the case moves to trial, where a jury is selected, opening statements are made, the prosecution presents its evidence, the defense presents its evidence, and both sides make closing arguments.  
The jury deliberates and returns a verdict of guilty or not guilty; if the verdict is guilty, the court proceeds to sentencing.  
During sentencing, the judge considers the United States Sentencing Guidelines, the nature of the offense, the defendant’s criminal history, and any mitigating or aggravating factors, and imposes a term of imprisonment, fines, restitution, or other penalties.  
The defendant has the right to appeal the conviction or sentence to the United States Court of Appeals for the circuit in which the district court sits, filing a notice of appeal within the statutory deadline.  
If the appellate court affirms the conviction and sentence, the defendant may seek further review by filing a petition for a writ of certiorari to the United States Supreme Court, which is discretionary and granted only in limited circumstances.  
Throughout the process, the defendant retains constitutional rights, including the right to counsel, the right against self‑incrimination, and the right to a speedy and public trial.
