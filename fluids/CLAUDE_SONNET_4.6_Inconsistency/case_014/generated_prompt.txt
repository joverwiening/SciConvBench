The flow has a Reynolds number of 0.1, confirming it is in the Stokes (creeping) flow regime.

Neglect the inertial (convective acceleration) terms in the Navier-Stokes equations, not the viscous terms.

The governing equations reduce to the Stokes equations: the pressure gradient balances the viscous diffusion term, written as grad(p) = mu * laplacian(u), combined with the incompressibility condition div(u) = 0.

Assume the canonical Stokes flow geometry: uniform flow past a solid sphere of radius R, which is the most well-known and well-validated benchmark problem for creeping flow.

The free-stream velocity U is uniform and directed along the z-axis (axial direction).

The fluid is incompressible and Newtonian with constant dynamic viscosity mu and constant density rho.

The Reynolds number is defined as Re = rho * U * 2R / mu = 0.1, confirming the Stokes flow assumption is valid.

Apply no-slip and no-penetration boundary conditions on the sphere surface (r = R): u = 0.

Apply the far-field boundary condition: the velocity field recovers to the uniform free-stream U as r approaches infinity.

Solve the Stokes equations in spherical coordinates (r, theta, phi) using the streamfunction approach, exploiting axisymmetry so there is no phi dependence.

The streamfunction satisfies the biharmonic equation: E^4(psi) = 0, where E^2 is the Stokes stream function operator in spherical coordinates.

The solution for the streamfunction is psi = U * sin^2(theta) * (r^2/2 - 3Rr/4 + R^3/(4r)).

Derive the velocity components u_r and u_theta from the streamfunction.

Derive the pressure field by integrating the Stokes momentum equation: p = p_inf - (3 * mu * U * R * cos(theta)) / (2 * r^2).

Compute the drag force on the sphere using the Stokes drag formula: F_drag = 6 * pi * mu * R * U.
