   k \;\ge\; 10\,\frac{E}{L_c}

2. **Adopt surface‑to‑surface contact** – This eliminates the artificial “single‑node” effect inherent to node‑to‑surface pairs and provides a smoother pressure distribution, especially important when the mesh is refined.

\[
k \;\ge\; 10\,\frac{E}{L_c}
\]

where \(L_c\) is the characteristic element length in the contact direction.  For a 0.25 m element, \(k ≈ 4 × 10⁶ N mm⁻³\) is advisable.  

5. **Controlled loading** – Apply the 100 MPa pressure in a *ramped* fashion (e.g., 0 → 100 MPa over 10 increments).  This gives the contact algorithm time to locate the interface before the pressure cap becomes active, improving robustness.  

6. **Result verification** – Export the **CSTRESS** field to a CSV (FILE, FORMAT=ASCII) and plot the pressure map (Python/Matplotlib or Abaqus/CAE).  Verify that the pressure cap is active everywhere and that no spurious penetration occurs (gap < 1 × 10⁻⁶ m).  

7. **Energy balance check** – Request *TOTAL ENERGY* and *INTERNAL ENERGY* in the STEP output.  Large discrepancies indicate an improperly defined contact law.

### 5.  Concluding remarks  

* With the **current penalty law** (k = 1 × 10⁶ N mm⁻³, cap = 3 MPa) the contact interface reaches the prescribed pressure limit almost instantly.  Consequently the master block only deforms enough to make contact, and the slave block exhibits **negligible displacement** (Δ ≤ 1 µm).  

* If the engineering intent is to study **elastic compression of both cubes** under the full 100 MPa load, the **pressure‑overclosure limit must be increased or removed**, the penalty stiffness should be raised, and a **surface‑to‑surface** contact definition with a **refined mesh** is strongly recommended.

1. **Raise or remove the pressure‑cap** – The present cap (3 MPa) is two orders of magnitude lower than the imposed 100 MPa. Set the cap to at least **30 MPa** or use a *hard‑contact* formulation (no cap) if the goal is to transmit the full load.  

2. **Adopt surface‑to‑surface contact** – Eliminates the artificial “single‑node” effect inherent to node‑to‑surface pairs and provides a smoother pressure distribution, especially important when the mesh is refined.  

3. **Mesh refinement at the interface** – Subdivide each 0.5 m cube into at least **2 × 2 × 2** elements (≈ 0.25 m element size) or finer. A finer mesh captures pressure gradients near edges and reduces sensitivity to the penalty stiffness.

where \(L_c\) is the characteristic element length in the contact direction. For a 0.25 m element, \(k ≈ 4 × 10⁶ N mm⁻³\) is advisable.  

5. **Controlled loading** – Apply the 100 MPa pressure in a *ramped* fashion (e.g., 0 → 100 MPa over 10 increments). This gives the contact algorithm time to locate the interface before the pressure cap becomes active, improving robustness.

* With the **current penalty law** (k = 1 × 10⁶ N mm⁻³, cap = 3 MPa) the contact interface reaches the prescribed pressure limit almost instantly. Consequently the master block only deforms enough to make contact, and the slave block exhibits **negligible displacement** (Δ ≤ 1 µm).

* Implementing the above recommendations will enable the model to transmit the applied pressure, generate a realistic pressure distribution, and produce measurable deformations of both blocks—allowing extraction of meaningful contact stresses and displacements for design verification or validation.

## 4.  Recommendations for a More Realistic / Higher‑Accuracy Study  

1. **Raise or eliminate the pressure‑cap** – The 3 MPa cap is two orders of magnitude lower than the applied 100 MPa. Set the cap to at least **30 MPa** or use a *hard‑contact* formulation (no cap) when the objective is to transmit the full load.  

2. **Use surface‑to‑surface contact** – This removes the artificial “single‑node” effect inherent to node‑to‑surface pairs and provides a smoother, more physically realistic pressure distribution, especially when the mesh is refined.  

3. **Refine the mesh at the interface** – Subdivide each 0.5 m cube into at least **2 × 2 × 2** elements (≈ 0.25 m element size) or finer. A finer mesh captures pressure gradients near edges and reduces sensitivity to the penalty stiffness.  

4. **Tune the penalty stiffness** – A practical guideline is  

\[
k \;\ge\; 10\,\frac{E}{L_c},
\]

where \(L_c\) is the characteristic element length in the contact direction. For a 0.25 m element, a value of **≈ 4 × 10⁶ N mm⁻³** is advisable.  

5. **Apply the load gradually** – Ramp the 100 MPa pressure over several increments (e.g., 0 → 100 MPa in 10 steps). This gives the contact algorithm time to locate the interface before the pressure cap becomes active, improving robustness.  

6. **Validate results** – Export the **CSTRESS** field to a CSV (FILE, FORMAT=ASCII) and plot the pressure map (Python/Matplotlib or Abaqus/CAE). Confirm that the pressure cap is active everywhere and that the gap remains < 1 × 10⁻⁶ m.  

7. **Check energy balance** – Request *TOTAL ENERGY* and *INTERNAL ENERGY* in the STEP output. Large discrepancies indicate an improperly defined contact law or numerical instability.

## 5.  Concluding Remarks  

With the present penalty formulation (**k = 1 × 10⁶ N mm⁻³**, **cap = 3 MPa**) the contact interface reaches the prescribed pressure limit almost instantly. Consequently the master block deforms only enough to establish contact, and the slave block exhibits **negligible displacement** (Δ ≤ 1 µm).  

If the engineering objective is to investigate the elastic compression of both cubes under the full 100 MPa load, the **pressure‑overclosure limit must be increased or removed**, the penalty stiffness should be raised, and a **surface‑to‑surface** contact definition with a **refined mesh** is strongly recommended.  

Implementing these recommendations will allow the model to transmit the applied pressure, generate a realistic pressure distribution, and produce measurable deformations of both blocks—enabling extraction of meaningful contact stresses and displacements for design verification or validation.
