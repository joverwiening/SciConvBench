**Why the two C3D20 bricks carry the forces shown in the table**

---

### 1.  What the solver “knows’’ about the model  

| Item | Value used by Abaqus | Where it comes from |
|------|----------------------|---------------------|
| **Material density** | \(\displaystyle \rho = 7.8\times10^{-9}\; \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{length}^{3}}\) | The *default* material that Abaqus creates automatically when no material is assigned. |
| **Element geometry** | Unit‑cube \([0,1]\times[0,1]\times[0,1]\) → \(V = 1\; \text{length}^{3}\) | The two bricks are defined as C3D20 (20‑node quadratic) bricks with side‑length = 1. |
| **Gravity vector** | \(\mathbf g = (0,0,-1)\) (magnitude = 1 in the –Z direction) | The *Static, General* step has gravity turned on by default. |

---

### 2.  Total body‑force on a single brick  

For a uniform body force the total load on the element is simply  

\[
\mathbf F_{\text{brick}} = \rho\,V\,\mathbf g
      = 7.8\times10^{-9}\;(0,0,-1)
      = (0,0,-7.8\times10^{-9}) .
\]

The magnitude \(|\mathbf F_{\text{brick}}| = 7.8\times10^{-9}\) is the **weight** that the element must support.

---

### 3.  From a uniform body force to equivalent nodal forces  

For a C3D20 (20‑node quadratic) brick the shape functions \(N_i\) are quadratic.  
If a constant body force \(\mathbf b\) (here \(\mathbf b = -\rho\mathbf g\)) acts on the element, the equivalent nodal force at node *i* is  

\[
\mathbf F_i = \int_{\Omega} N_i\,\mathbf b \; d\Omega .
\]

Because \(\mathbf b\) is constant, the integral reduces to  

\[
\mathbf F_i = \mathbf b \int_{\Omega} N_i \, d\Omega .
\]

The integrals of the shape functions over a unit brick are the **nodal weights**  

| Node type | \(\displaystyle\int_{\Omega} N_i d\Omega\) | Weight \(w_i\) |
|-----------|--------------------------------------------|----------------|
| Corner node (8 of them) | 2 | \(w_c = 2\) |
| Mid‑edge node (12 of them) | 1 | \(w_m = 1\) |

The sum of all weights for a C3D20 element is  

\[
\sum_{j=1}^{20} w_j = 8\times2 + 12\times1 = 28 .
\]

Hence the **fraction of the total body force** that each node receives is  

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
\mathbf F_i = \mathbf b \; \frac{w_i}{\sum_j w_j}
          = -\,\rho V \; \frac{w_i}{28}\,\mathbf e_z } .
\]

---

### 4.  Numerical nodal forces for one brick  

| Node type | Formula | Z‑component (force‑unit) |
|-----------|---------|--------------------------|
| Corner (8) | \(-\rho V \dfrac{2}{28}= -\dfrac{\rho V}{14}\) | \(-1.0\times10^{-9}\) |
| Mid‑edge (12) | \(-\rho V \dfrac{1}{28}= -\dfrac{\rho V}{28}\) | \(-5.0\times10^{-10}\) |

All X‑ and Y‑components are zero because the gravity vector has no X/Y part.

---

### 5.  Check – they add up to the total weight  

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{\text{corners}}F_z &= 8\Bigl(-\frac{\rho V}{14}\Bigr)= -\frac{8}{14}\rho V,\\[2mm]
\sum_{\text{mid‑edges}}F_z &= 12\Bigl(-\frac{\rho V}{28}\Bigr)= -\frac{12}{28}\rho V,\\[2mm]
\text{Total} &= -\rho V .
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus the 20 equivalent nodal forces reproduce exactly the gravity load on the element.

---

### 6.  Two bricks together  

Because the two bricks are identical, the forces are simply doubled:

* **Total gravity load on the model**: \(-2\rho V = -1.56\times10^{-8}\) (‑Z).  
* **Each corner node** (there are 16 of them) carries \(-1.0\times10^{-9}\).  
* **Each midside node** (there are 24 of them) carries \(-5.0\times10^{-10}\).

These are precisely the numbers that appear in the table you were given.

---

## How to see those forces in Abaqus/CAE  

| Step | Action in CAE | What you will see |
|------|----------------|-------------------|
| **1. Verify the gravity load** | *Step → Edit → Loads → Gravity* – make sure the vector is \((0,0,-1)\). | If it was turned off, turn it on and re‑run the step. |
| **2. Run the analysis** | *Job → Submit → OK*. | An *.odb* file is created. |
| **3. Open the output database** | *Result → Open* → select the *.odb*. | The Visualization module opens. |
| **4. Display nodal reaction forces (RF)** | *Display → Field Output → Nodal* → choose **RF** → set **Component → Z** (or *Magnitude*). | A contour plot shows the Z‑reaction at every node. |
| **5. Probe a single node** | Click the **Probe Values** (i‑icon) and then a node. | The dialog lists **RF1, RF2, RF3**; for a corner node you will read ≈ ‑1.0 e‑9, for a midside node ≈ ‑5.0 e‑10. |
| **6. Export the whole field (optional)** | *Report → Field Output* → select **RF**, choose *ASCII* or *CSV*, give a file name → **Write**. | The file contains one line per node with the three components of the reaction force; you can post‑process it in Excel, Python, etc. |

> **Note:** The reaction forces (RF) are the *nodal forces that the solver applied to enforce equilibrium*. Because the only external load is gravity, the RF values are exactly the equivalent nodal forces derived above.

---

### Bottom line  

The forces on the two 20‑node bricks are not arbitrary; they are the mathematically exact distribution of the uniform gravity body force to the 20 quadratic shape functions of each C3D20 element. The distribution gives a weight of 2 to each corner node and 1 to each midside node, which leads to the forces \(-1.0\times10^{-9}\) (corner) and \(-5.0\times10^{-10}\) (mid‑edge) in the –Z direction.  

You can **inspect** these forces directly in Abaqus/CAE by displaying the **RF** field (Z‑component) in the Visualization module, probing individual nodes, or exporting the field with *Report → Field Output*. The values you read will match the table you were given.
